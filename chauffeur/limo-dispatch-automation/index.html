Dispatch automation · the intake layer

Dispatch automation that starts at the phone.

AVA automates the intake layer of dispatch — answering every call, capturing the whole trip, quoting from your own rate rules, and handing over a structured ticket. It does not automate the decisions. Your dispatcher still assigns the vehicle, confirms the trip, and runs the operation, exactly as they do now.

Last updated 7 August 2026

Which layer

Dispatch is two jobs. Only one of them is typing.

Most of what gets sold as dispatch automation tries to automate the decisions. This automates the typing, the answering and the asking — and leaves the decisions where they belong.

Watch a dispatcher for an hour and the work splits cleanly in half. One half is intake: pick up, ask the same nine questions, write the answers down, quote a range, and get it in front of somebody. The other half is judgement — which car, which driver, what to do when a flight moves, whether this one goes to an affiliate.

The first half is identical on every call and it is where the hours go. The second half is why you hired the person. Automating the first does not touch the second, and any product that claims to automate both is describing a business that does not have exceptions in it.

Automated · the intake layer
Answering, asking, writing down, quoting, handing over. Every call, every line at once, whatever time it is. AVA takes pickup, dropoff, date and time, vehicle class, passengers, bags, flight and terminal, callback number and notes — then produces a structured ticket.
Not automated · the decisions
Which vehicle, which driver, and whether to take the job at all. Your dispatcher confirms every trip. AVA does not assign a car, does not commit a vehicle, does not take payment, and does not send work out to an affiliate.
Where the two meet
The ticket. It is the handoff between the layer that runs itself and the layer that never will, which is why it carries the whole trip instead of a summary — the person making the decision should not have to call anyone back to make it.

What the seat costs

A dispatcher is expensive. So is a phone nobody answers.

A full-time dispatcher runs roughly $45,000 to $60,000 a year in most US markets once payroll taxes and benefits are counted — the loaded cost of the seat, not the number on the offer letter. That is a general market range for the role, not a quote and not a figure from anybody's books.

The seat is worth it. What it does not do is cover 168 hours a week, and the hours it cannot cover are the ones where the calls are hardest to replace: a Friday at 11 PM, a Sunday morning, the forty minutes your dispatcher is on another line.

AVA covers the intake layer of that job around the clock. This is not a headcount argument. AVA does not replace a dispatcher, and nothing on this site will tell you it does — it takes the repetitive half of the work off the seat so the person in it spends their shift on the half that needs a person. What that is worth to your operation depends on your call volume and your close rate, and neither of those is a number we are going to invent for you.

FAQ

Questions operators actually ask.

What part of dispatch does AVA actually automate?
The intake layer, and only that: answering the line, asking the whole set of questions, writing the answers down, quoting a range from your configured rate rules, and handing over a structured ticket. Everything that follows — which car, which driver, whether to take the job — stays with your dispatcher.
Does automated intake mean trips book themselves?
No. A captured trip is a request written down properly, not a commitment. Your dispatcher confirms every one of them, and AVA does not commit a vehicle or take payment. On platforms where direct write-back has been configured for your account, the record is created for you — and it still goes through the same approval your operation uses today.
What does a dispatcher stop doing?
Reading voicemail back to themselves, asking the same nine questions for the eleventh time that shift, and calling people back for a bag count. What they keep is the work that needed them in the first place. Nobody's job disappears here; the repetitive half of it does.
Does this work if we already have someone answering the phone?
Yes, and that is the common case. The intake layer runs underneath the person, not instead of them — it takes the overflow when two lines ring at once, and the hours nobody is sitting there. Where AVA sits on your lines is one of the things decided on the setup call.
